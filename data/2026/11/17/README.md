We’ve developed a forearm sleeve that reads muscle activity to decode finger movements and estimate fingertip force, while leaving the hands bare. It’s used to record human movement and generate training data for dextrous robots. Here’s a demo video: https://youtube.com/shorts/xBrocTc9cfA We’ve found that electromyography (EMG) is a much better way to train robots than by video alone because video only captures movement, not muscle activity or the effort behind the movement. A useful signal can be present even when the hand barely moves; just maintaining a grip involves muscle activation. The subtle muscle movements that can barely be picked up in video are the most important details for activities like sewing and delicate assembly. The sleeve has 64 channels of surface electromyography, or EMG. Electrodes on the skin detect electrical activity from muscles in the forearm. We distribute sensors across the forearm so as to not overfit to one muscle group. We synchronize EMG with hand tracking to train a model that predicts finger joint angles, then we run the decoder solely from EMG, adding in fingertip force estimates that have been calibrated from finger presses on force sensors. When the user takes the sleeve off then puts it back on again, the electrode positions change, so we run tests over separate wearings. In one case, we ran a 215-second recording, four days after training, without any recalibration, and compared the results with Apple Vision Pro hand tracking. The result was just 6.9° error across 20 joint angles. We’ve published the measurements and evaluation details here: https://axonnic.com/benchmarks . We faced many challenges doing recordings in factories, including wireless interference and unreliable off-the-shelf hardware. This led to some bad recordings and the need to repeat data collection. For that reason, we’re developing our own electronics that use a wired PCB design that will eliminate interference and timing inconsistencies. Axonnic grew out of Max's EMG research at Oxford. We looked at using the technology for computer control, then we thought a more promising use-case was in capturing the effort involved in skilled manual tasks. This technology could be described as a computer to human interface. Where computers gain insight and learn from human skills. Sewing particularly stood out because the material deforms and the operator is constantly adjusting grip and tension. Maxim has an Oxford MBiol and conducted research on high-density surface EMG.
